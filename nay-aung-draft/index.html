Cardiac MRI

Seeing how well your heart muscle works

With every beat, your heart muscle shortens from top to bottom and squeezes inwards, a little like wringing out a towel. Cardiac MRI can measure this movement precisely. In the images, brighter colours mean stronger contraction: a healthy heart glows, while a weakened heart stays darker. These measurements can show changes in the heart muscle earlier than standard tests.

Top: the heart muscle shortening, seen from three angles. Bottom: the heart squeezing inwards, a healthy heart (left) beside a heart with heart failure (right).

What your heart muscle is made of

Beyond how well the heart pumps, cardiac MRI can look inside the muscle itself, without a biopsy or radiation. It shows scarring after a heart attack, diffuse scarring in conditions such as cardiomyopathy, and swelling from inflammation such as myocarditis.

Scar

After a contrast injection, scarred muscle lights up. Here a bright band along the inner lining of the wall, from a past heart attack.

T1 map

Each colour is a measurement. Fibrosis and conditions such as amyloidosis change the muscle's value.

T2 map

Measures water in the muscle, so it picks up swelling from inflammation or a recent injury.

Research that shapes my practice

My research uses cardiac MRI, genetics and AI to detect heart disease earlier. It has led to over 150 peer-reviewed papers, and it shapes how I care for my patients.

Air pollution and the heart

In nearly 4,000 people, I found that higher exposure to traffic pollution was linked to enlarged heart chambers, a change also seen in early heart failure. Covered by BBC News and the Guardian.

Read the studyBBC News

Detecting heart failure from a routine ECG

I lead iHeF, an AI tool that spots signs of heart failure on a standard ECG, a quick test available in any GP surgery. In 2026 it received £1.3m from the NIHR to prepare it for NHS use.

Queen Mary newsBarts Health news

AI for heart imaging

AI can outline every heart chamber on MRI and build a 3D model in seconds. Using it on 17,000 scans, my team found 14 genetic regions linked to heart size and function, pointing to new ways to predict heart failure.
